# Annotated Bibliography: Should Central Banks Publish the Policy Rate Path?

**Prepared by:** librarian agent
**Date:** 2026-03-13
**Research question:** Should central banks publish the policy rate path? — theoretical and empirical foundations.

---

## Category 1: Theoretical Foundations — Commitment vs. Discretion

### 1.1 Kydland & Prescott (1977)

**Kydland, Finn E., and Edward C. Prescott. "Rules Rather than Discretion: The Inconsistency of Optimal Plans." *Journal of Political Economy* 85, no. 3 (1977): 473–492.**

Foundational paper establishing the time-inconsistency problem in macroeconomic policy. Shows that even a benevolent policymaker with a rational objective function will produce suboptimal outcomes under discretion because agents anticipate the incentive to deviate from pre-announced plans. In monetary policy, this generates an "inflation bias": the central bank, wishing to reduce unemployment below its natural rate, over-inflates relative to the social optimum. The direct implication for rate-path publication is that any announced path lacks credibility unless accompanied by a commitment device — the policy implication underpins the entire forward guidance debate.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical (dynamic programming, game theory)
- **Key data source:** None (theoretical)
- **Main result:** Discretionary optimal policy is time-inconsistent; rules dominate discretion
- **Proximity score:** 3 (foundational theoretical motivation)

---

### 1.2 Barro & Gordon (1983)

**Barro, Robert J., and David B. Gordon. "Rules, Discretion and Reputation in a Model of Monetary Policy." *Journal of Monetary Economics* 12, no. 1 (1983): 101–121.**

Develops the reputation equilibrium as an alternative to formal commitment rules. Shows that a discretionary central bank produces an equilibrium with persistently higher inflation than the social optimum (the "inflationary bias"), but that reputational forces in repeated interactions can partially substitute for binding rules. The paper establishes the theoretical background for why communication strategies — including rate-path publication — may build credibility and replicate commitment outcomes without formal constitutional constraints.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical (repeated game equilibrium)
- **Key data source:** None (theoretical)
- **Main result:** Discretionary equilibrium features inflation bias; reputation can partially mitigate it
- **Proximity score:** 3 (foundational)

---

### 1.3 Woodford (2003)

**Woodford, Michael. *Interest and Prices: Foundations of a Theory of Monetary Policy.* Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2003.**

This monograph provides the comprehensive New Keynesian framework that underlies modern forward guidance analysis. The chapter "Central Banking as Management of Expectations" argues that monetary policy works primarily through shaping private-sector expectations about the future path of interest rates, not through the contemporaneous level of the overnight rate. Woodford demonstrates that commitment to the right history-dependent policy rule, communicated credibly to the public, can substantially improve welfare. The book is the primary theoretical reference justifying rate-path publication as an expectations-management tool.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical (dynamic optimization, New Keynesian DSGE)
- **Key data source:** None (theoretical)
- **Main result:** Policy expectations, not just current rates, determine equilibrium outcomes; commitment is valuable
- **Proximity score:** 4 (core theoretical framework for the paper's motivation)

---

### 1.4 Eggertsson & Woodford (2003)

**Eggertsson, Gauti B., and Michael Woodford. "The Zero Bound on Interest Rates and Optimal Monetary Policy." *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* 34, no. 1 (2003): 139–235.**

Seminal paper on optimal monetary policy when the nominal interest rate is constrained at the zero lower bound (ZLB). Shows that unconventional open-market operations are ineffective absent changes in expectations about the future policy path. The key result is that committing to keep rates "lower for longer" — i.e., providing forward guidance through a credible policy-rate path — can partially substitute for the lost room for conventional stimulus. This paper provides the primary theoretical rationale for quantitative forward guidance at the ZLB.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical (New Keynesian DSGE with ZLB, Markov switching)
- **Key data source:** None (theoretical)
- **Main result:** Credible commitment to future low rates is the key policy tool at the ZLB; current open-market operations are irrelevant without it
- **Proximity score:** 5 (directly motivates rate-path publication at the ZLB; core reference)

---

## Category 2: Arguments FOR Publishing the Rate Path

### 2.1 Geraats (2002)

**Geraats, Petra M. "Central Bank Transparency." *Economic Journal* 112, no. 483 (2002): F532–F565.**

Comprehensive survey establishing the five-dimensional taxonomy of central bank transparency — political, economic, procedural, policy, and operational — that has become the field's benchmark measurement framework (adopted in Eijffinger & Geraats, 2006; Dincer, Eichengreen & Geraats, 2022). Distinguishes uncertainty effects from incentive effects of transparency. Argues that greater transparency generally improves welfare by disciplining the central bank and improving private-sector inference. The paper provides the theoretical scaffolding for evaluating rate-path publication as one dimension of "policy transparency."

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical survey with formal modeling
- **Key data source:** Survey of central bank practices
- **Main result:** Transparency is generally welfare-enhancing; five-dimensional taxonomy proposed
- **Proximity score:** 4 (core reference for transparency benefits argument)

---

### 2.2 Woodford (2005)

**Woodford, Michael. "Central Bank Communication and Policy Effectiveness." NBER Working Paper No. 11898, 2005.**

Directly addresses the question of whether central banks should communicate about the likely path of future interest rates, with particular reference to the FOMC's experiment with policy signaling post-August 2003. Provides evidence that the Fed required smaller open-market operations after 1994 due to greater transparency, arguing that advance signaling makes it easier for the central bank to move rates to desired levels. This is the key "pro-publication" paper from Woodford, complementing his theoretical work in *Interest and Prices*.

- **Identification strategy:** Descriptive/theoretical; event study of FOMC communication
- **Key data source:** FOMC statements, federal funds futures
- **Main result:** Communication about the future rate path improves policy transmission; the "extended period" language reduced the required scale of open-market operations
- **Proximity score:** 5 (directly argues for rate-path publication)

---

### 2.3 Faust & Svensson (2001)

**Faust, Jon, and Lars E.O. Svensson. "Transparency and Credibility: Monetary Policy with Unobservable Goals." *International Economic Review* 42, no. 2 (2001): 369–397.**

Develops a formal model in which the central bank's objectives are not directly observable by the public. Shows that greater transparency — making policy intentions and objectives more visible — makes the central bank's reputation more sensitive to its actions, which disciplines it toward the socially optimal policy. Greater transparency generally benefits society even though it may reduce the central bank's own welfare (by reducing its flexibility). Key theoretical paper supporting the case for publishing rate paths as an accountability mechanism.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical (signaling game with reputation)
- **Key data source:** None (theoretical)
- **Main result:** Transparency improves credibility and welfare by disciplining central bank behavior
- **Proximity score:** 4 (core theoretical support for transparency argument)

---

### 2.4 Morris & Shin (2002) and Svensson (2006)

**Morris, Stephen, and Hyun Song Shin. "The Social Value of Public Information." *American Economic Review* 92, no. 5 (2002): 1521–1534.**

**Svensson, Lars E.O. "Social Value of Public Information: Comment: Morris and Shin (2002) Is Actually Pro Transparency, Not Con." *American Economic Review* 96, no. 1 (2006): 448–452.**

Morris & Shin present an influential theoretical result showing that, in a beauty-contest framework with strategic complementarity, greater public information precision can harm welfare by causing excessive coordination on public signals and crowding out private information. This was interpreted as an anti-transparency result with direct implications for rate-path publication. Svensson's response demonstrates that the anti-transparency conclusion requires implausibly imprecise public information — for empirically plausible parameter values, more public information is welfare-enhancing. These two papers together define the boundary of the theoretical debate about information provision.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical (global games, beauty-contest framework)
- **Key data source:** None (theoretical)
- **Main result (Morris-Shin):** More public information can harm welfare; (Svensson response:) the condition for this requires implausibly low public signal precision
- **Proximity score:** 4 (core theoretical debate about whether publishing rate paths can backfire)

---

### 2.5 Svensson (2015)

**Svensson, Lars E.O. "Forward Guidance." *International Journal of Central Banking* 11 (Supplement 1), (2015): 19–64. NBER Working Paper No. 20796.**

Direct empirical assessment of rate-path publication by the three early-adopter central banks — Riksbank (from 2007), RBNZ (from 1997), and the Federal Reserve's dot plot (from 2012). Examines both predictability (did markets anticipate the published path?) and credibility (did market expectations align with the published path after release?). Documents a striking case of Riksbank failure: a large divergence between a high-and-rising Riksbank path and a low-and-falling market path, where the market proved correct. Concludes that paths have moderate credibility but can be misleading when the central bank pursues secondary objectives (such as the Riksbank's "leaning against the wind" policy).

- **Identification strategy:** Event study, comparison of central bank path vs. market path (OIS rates)
- **Key data source:** Riksbank, RBNZ, Fed SEP projections; overnight index swap (OIS) curves
- **Main result:** Rate paths have moderate credibility and predictability; paths can mislead when central bank pursues non-transparent secondary objectives
- **Proximity score:** 5 (most directly comparable paper to the proposed research)

---

### 2.6 Campbell, Evans, Fisher & Justiniano (2012)

**Campbell, Jeffrey R., Charles L. Evans, Jonas D.M. Fisher, and Alejandro Justiniano. "Macroeconomic Effects of Federal Reserve Forward Guidance." *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* 43, no. 1 (2012): 1–80.**

Introduces the influential distinction between Delphic forward guidance (forecasting future macroeconomic outcomes and policy under the normal reaction function) and Odyssean forward guidance (committing to deviate from the normal reaction function). Uses DSGE model analysis and event studies to show that Odyssean guidance — essentially the publication of a committed rate path that differs from what the policy rule would otherwise dictate — is both feasible and effective. This paper provides the conceptual framework that underpins most subsequent empirical work on rate-path effects.

- **Identification strategy:** DSGE model estimation + event study analysis
- **Key data source:** FOMC statements, federal funds futures, Blue Chip forecasts
- **Main result:** Odyssean (commitment) forward guidance is effective; markets have already incorporated most FOMC forward guidance as Odyssean pre-crisis
- **Proximity score:** 5 (foundational empirical/conceptual paper for research question)

## Category 3: Arguments AGAINST Publishing the Rate Path

### 3.1 Mishkin (2004)

**Mishkin, Frederic S. "Can Central Bank Transparency Go Too Far?" NBER Working Paper No. 10829, 2004.**

Direct engagement with the question of whether there is a limit to beneficial transparency. Argues that while discretion creates an inflation bias, central banks need not choose between rigid rules and pure discretion — "constrained discretion" (Bernanke and Mishkin's framing) allows rule-like behavior without the rigidity that prevents response to new information. Concludes that excessive forward commitment — such as a published rate path perceived as a binding promise — reduces the central bank's ability to respond to unanticipated shocks, especially during financial disruptions.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical/policy analysis
- **Key data source:** Historical episodes of central bank forward guidance
- **Main result:** Transparency can go too far when it reduces flexibility; constrained discretion is preferable to full commitment
- **Proximity score:** 4 (key paper in the anti-publication case)

---

### 3.2 Feroli, Greenlaw, Hooper, Mishkin & Sufi (2017)

**Feroli, Michael, David Greenlaw, Peter Hooper, Frederic S. Mishkin, and Amir Sufi. "Language after Liftoff: Fed Communication Away from the Zero Lower Bound." *Research in Economics* 71, no. 3 (2017): 452–490.**

Evaluates the Fed's communication strategy post-ZLB and finds that the FOMC relied excessively on time-based forward guidance ("rates will remain low at least through mid-2013") rather than state-contingent, data-based guidance. Time-based guidance mutes the effect of macroeconomic news on interest rates, unnecessarily constrains future Fed action, and generates confusion when the path is not honored. Argues for clear communication of the reaction function rather than explicit interest rate projections. This paper is the most direct academic critique of the Fed's dot plot approach.

- **Identification strategy:** Event study; regression of forward rate changes on macroeconomic data releases
- **Key data source:** FOMC statements, federal funds futures, Eurodollar futures, economic data releases
- **Main result:** Time-based forward guidance (and by extension the dot plot) is inferior to state-contingent (rule-based) communication; the former constrains flexibility without benefit
- **Proximity score:** 5 (directly argues against the dot plot and rate-path publication)

---

### 3.3 Andersson & Hofmann (2010)

**Andersson, Magnus, and Boris Hofmann. "Gauging the Effectiveness of Quantitative Forward Guidance: Evidence from Three Inflation Targeters." ECB Working Paper No. 1098, 2009. Published as working paper.**

Compares quantitative (rate-path) vs. qualitative (verbal) forward guidance for the three early-adopter central banks (RBNZ, Norges Bank, Riksbank). Finds that asset price responses to RBNZ meetings with published rate paths are very similar to those without, suggesting the marginal contribution of quantitative forward guidance over qualitative guidance is small. This is an important challenge to the rate-path-publication hypothesis: markets may extract forward guidance from text and context without needing a numerical path.

- **Identification strategy:** Event study comparing announcement-day yield responses (with vs. without path publication)
- **Key data source:** RBNZ, Norges Bank, Riksbank announcement data; government bond yields
- **Main result:** Quantitative rate paths add little marginal information over qualitative guidance
- **Proximity score:** 4 (directly tests the incremental value of rate-path publication)

---

## Category 4: The Forward Guidance Puzzle and Resolution

### 4.1 Del Negro, Giannoni & Patterson (2023)

**Del Negro, Marco, Marc P. Giannoni, and Christina Patterson. "The Forward Guidance Puzzle." *Journal of Political Economy Macroeconomics* 1, no. 1 (2023): 43–79.**

The paper that defines and quantifies the forward guidance puzzle: standard DSGE models predict effects of forward guidance (e.g., an announcement of zero rates for two years) that are 28 times larger for output and 8 times larger for inflation than empirically observed. Documents the puzzle using Blue Chip survey data. Proposes perpetual youth (overlapping-generations) structure as one resolution, which discounts far-future promises at the rate of mortality. This paper frames the major challenge for theoretical models of forward guidance effectiveness — and therefore for the policy case for publishing long-horizon rate paths.

- **Identification strategy:** Structural DSGE model matched to Blue Chip survey forecasts
- **Key data source:** Blue Chip Economic Indicators survey; FOMC announcement data
- **Main result:** Standard DSGE models drastically overpredict the power of forward guidance; perpetual-youth discounting partially resolves the puzzle
- **Proximity score:** 4 (defines the theoretical puzzle that limits the case for rate-path publication)

---

### 4.2 McKay, Nakamura & Steinsson (2016)

**McKay, Alisdair, Emi Nakamura, and Jón Steinsson. "The Power of Forward Guidance Revisited." *American Economic Review* 106, no. 10 (2016): 3133–3158.**

Shows that the forward guidance puzzle disappears when agents face incomplete markets with uninsurable income risk and borrowing constraints. In incomplete-markets models, precautionary savings dampen the response to changes in future interest rates — households facing liquidity constraints respond less to promises about distant interest rates because they cannot borrow against future income gains. This is a resolution via heterogeneous agents that also has policy implications: forward guidance is less powerful than standard models suggest, which means its benefits need to be weighed against the costs of commitment.

- **Identification strategy:** Structural HANK model (heterogeneous agents New Keynesian)
- **Key data source:** Model-based (calibrated to U.S. data)
- **Main result:** Incomplete markets substantially reduce the power of forward guidance; precautionary savings generate discounting of far-future promises
- **Proximity score:** 4 (key theoretical resolution, directly relevant to policy case)

---

### 4.3 Angeletos & Lian (2018)

**Angeletos, George-Marios, and Chen Lian. "Forward Guidance without Common Knowledge." *American Economic Review* 108, no. 9 (2018): 2477–2512.**

Relaxes the assumption that agents have common knowledge of policy announcements — instead, agents face higher-order uncertainty about how others will respond. Shows that the absence of common knowledge attenuates general equilibrium effects of forward guidance by an increasing function of the horizon. As T increases, the effectiveness of forward guidance diminishes because each round of strategic forecasting introduces an additional discount factor. This resolves the forward guidance puzzle via an information-friction mechanism and also provides a rationale for myopia at the aggregate level, with direct implications for the policy relevance of long-horizon rate paths.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical (incomplete information game, New Keynesian extension)
- **Key data source:** None (theoretical)
- **Main result:** Lack of common knowledge attenuates GE effects of forward guidance, with attenuation growing with horizon; resolves the puzzle without full heterogeneous agents
- **Proximity score:** 4 (directly relevant to horizon-dependence of rate-path effects)

---

### 4.4 McKay & Wieland (2022)

**McKay, Alisdair, and Johannes F. Wieland. "Lumpy Durable Consumption Demand and the Limited Responsiveness of Output to Monetary Policy." NBER Working Paper. [Published version: *Journal of Political Economy* 130, no. 11 (2022): 2878–2936.]**

Shows that including durable goods in a HANK model substantially reduces the power of forward guidance. Durable goods prices are sensitive to the user cost (current real rate) rather than cumulative future rates, so promises about distant future rates have minimal effect on current durable spending. This provides an alternative — and arguably more empirically grounded — resolution to the forward guidance puzzle that also limits the scope for long-horizon rate-path publication to affect real activity.

- **Identification strategy:** Structural model with lumpy durable goods investment
- **Key data source:** Calibrated to U.S. household survey data
- **Main result:** Durable goods substantially reduce forward guidance power; provides a novel resolution to the puzzle
- **Proximity score:** 3 (related theoretical resolution, relevant to policy case)

---

## Category 5: Neo-Fisherian Critique

### 5.1 Cochrane (2018)

**Cochrane, John H. "Michelson-Morley, Fisher, and Occam: The Radical Implications of Stable Quiet Inflation at the Zero Bound." *NBER Macroeconomics Annual* 32 (2018): 113–226.**

Argues that stable, quiet inflation at near-zero rates during 2010–2015 is inconsistent with standard New Keynesian models and their forward guidance prescriptions. In the standard NK model, an interest rate peg is explosive without implicit off-equilibrium threats; the fiscal theory of the price level provides a more coherent alternative. The neo-Fisherian implication: pegging rates at zero for an extended period will eventually *lower* inflation, contrary to the central bank's intentions — a challenge to the "lower for longer" forward guidance strategy. Directly relevant to the theoretical foundations of long-horizon rate-path publication.

- **Identification strategy:** Theoretical/empirical (calibration, historical narrative)
- **Key data source:** U.S. CPI, Federal Reserve balance sheet data
- **Main result:** Standard NK equilibrium selection at the ZLB relies on implausible off-equilibrium threats; fiscal theory provides an alternative in which extended low rates eventually reduce, not raise, inflation
- **Proximity score:** 3 (challenges the theoretical case for rate-path publication at ZLB)

---

## Category 6: High-Frequency Identification of Guidance Shocks

### 6.1 Gürkaynak, Sack & Swanson (2005)

**Gürkaynak, Refet S., Brian Sack, and Eric T. Swanson. "Do Actions Speak Louder Than Words? The Response of Asset Prices to Monetary Policy Actions and Statements." *International Journal of Central Banking* 1, no. 1 (2005): 55–93.**

Foundational paper decomposing high-frequency interest rate changes around FOMC announcements into two factors: a "target" factor (immediate rate change) and a "path" factor (surprise in the future rate path, driven primarily by FOMC statements). Shows that FOMC statements have substantially larger effects on longer-term Treasury yields than on the current rate — the path factor explains most of the yield curve response. Establishes the empirical foundation for separately identifying forward guidance shocks, and demonstrates that statements are, in the language of later work, "at least as important" as rate actions.

- **Identification strategy:** Factor analysis on high-frequency (intraday 30-min) interest rate changes around FOMC announcements
- **Key data source:** Fed funds futures, Eurodollar futures, Treasury yields; FOMC announcement windows
- **Main result:** FOMC statements (path factor) have larger effects on medium- and long-term yields than the rate action itself (target factor); two-factor model is sufficient
- **Proximity score:** 5 (the canonical empirical method for identifying forward guidance shocks)

---

### 6.2 Nakamura & Steinsson (2018)

**Nakamura, Emi, and Jón Steinsson. "High-Frequency Identification of Monetary Non-Neutrality: The Information Effect." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 133, no. 3 (2018): 1283–1330.**

Uses 30-minute windows around scheduled FOMC announcements to identify monetary policy surprises. Finds a puzzling result: surprise interest rate hikes raise real interest rates and also raise expected output growth — the opposite of what standard models predict. Interprets this as an "information effect": when the Fed raises rates, markets revise upward their assessment of the Fed's private information about the strength of the economy. This central-bank information effect is a key confounding factor for interpreting forward guidance announcements and complicates the use of rate paths as pure policy signals.

- **Identification strategy:** High-frequency event study (30-minute window around FOMC announcements); discontinuity-based identification
- **Key data source:** TIPS-based real rates, inflation expectations, Blue Chip GDP forecasts; FOMC announcements
- **Main result:** Rate hikes raise real rates and increase expected output; the "information effect" channel is quantitatively important
- **Proximity score:** 4 (key empirical paper; the information effect confounds forward guidance signal)

---

### 6.3 Jarociński & Karadi (2020)

**Jarociński, Marek, and Peter Karadi. "Deconstructing Monetary Policy Surprises — The Role of Information Shocks." *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 12, no. 2 (2020): 1–43.**

Decomposes high-frequency interest rate surprises around FOMC announcements into two orthogonal components using the co-movement of interest rates and stock prices: a pure monetary policy shock (rates and stocks move in opposite directions) and a central bank information shock (rates and stocks move in the same direction, because higher rates signal better economic news). Shows that ignoring the information shock biases inference about monetary policy non-neutrality. Directly relevant to interpreting forward guidance announcements: a published rate path may be read as Delphic (central bank forecast) rather than Odyssean (commitment), contaminating its effects with an information shock.

- **Identification strategy:** High-frequency factor analysis; sign restriction to separate policy vs. information shocks
- **Key data source:** Fed funds futures, S&P 500; FOMC announcement windows 1979–2019
- **Main result:** Information shocks are quantitatively important and bias standard HFI estimates; the two shocks have opposite macroeconomic effects
- **Proximity score:** 4 (methodological foundation for separating commitment from information effects of rate-path publication)

---

### 6.4 Swanson (2021)

**Swanson, Eric T. "Measuring the Effects of Federal Reserve Forward Guidance and Asset Purchases on Financial Markets." *Journal of Monetary Economics* 118 (2021): 32–53.**

Extends Gürkaynak et al. (2005) to separately identify three policy factors: federal funds rate changes, forward guidance, and large-scale asset purchases (LSAPs). Uses principal component analysis on high-frequency asset price responses to FOMC announcements 1991–2019, rotated to match the three policy instruments. Finds that forward guidance and LSAPs both had substantial, statistically significant effects on Treasury yields, corporate bond yields, stock prices, and exchange rates — comparable in magnitude to conventional policy. This is the key paper for separately measuring the market impact of rate-path announcements.

- **Identification strategy:** Factor analysis (PCA + rotation) on 30-minute asset price changes around FOMC announcements
- **Key data source:** Fed funds futures, Eurodollar futures, Treasury yields, S&P 500; FOMC announcements 1991–2019
- **Main result:** Forward guidance has significant effects on the full term structure; comparable in magnitude to conventional rate changes; LSAPs have smaller effects
- **Proximity score:** 5 (core empirical method and benchmark for measuring rate-path effects)

---

### 6.5 Bauer & Swanson (2023)

**Bauer, Michael D., and Eric T. Swanson. "A Reassessment of Monetary Policy Surprises and High-Frequency Identification." *NBER Macroeconomics Annual* 37 (2023): 87–155.**

Documents that standard high-frequency monetary policy surprises are predictable from publicly available macroeconomic data, creating endogeneity that biases standard estimates. Proposes two fixes: (1) expand announcements to include Fed Chair speeches, and (2) orthogonalize surprises against publicly available information (the "Fed response to news" correction). Finds corrected estimates of macroeconomic effects up to four times larger than naive HFI estimates. The paper also challenges the "Fed information effect" interpretation of Nakamura & Steinsson (2018), arguing that the predictability is driven by systematic policy responses to observed data, not private Fed information. Essential methodological reference for any empirical work on forward guidance shocks.

- **Identification strategy:** IV with endogeneity-corrected high-frequency monetary policy surprises
- **Key data source:** Federal funds futures, Eurodollar futures, Fed Chair speeches; macroeconomic data releases
- **Main result:** Standard HFI estimates are biased downward by the Fed-response-to-news channel; corrected estimates are 2–4x larger; no evidence of important Fed information effects
- **Proximity score:** 4 (key methodological reference)

---

## Category 7: Central Bank Experience — Early Adopters

### 7.1 BIS Working Paper (Brubakk, ter Ellen & Xu, 2017) / Natvik, Rime & Syrstad (2019)

**Key cross-country BIS evidence on Norges Bank, Riksbank, and RBNZ rate-path publication.**

A series of BIS working papers (including BIS WP 742, 1009, and the Norges Bank Papers 2024) examines the experience of the three early-adopter central banks. Key findings: (1) Norges Bank's published path is approximately 75% predictable from market information the day before release; (2) market rates adjust significantly to published path surprises but far less than one-for-one; (3) credibility decreases with horizon; (4) for the RBNZ, the marginal contribution of quantitative path information over qualitative guidance is small. These papers provide the empirical baseline against which any new study must be positioned.

- **Identification strategy:** Event study; regression of yield changes on path surprises
- **Key data source:** Norges Bank, Riksbank, RBNZ path publications; OIS curves; government bond yields
- **Main result:** Rate paths are partially predictable and partially credible; credibility falls with horizon; quantitative paths add limited marginal information over verbal guidance
- **Proximity score:** 5 (closest empirical precedent to the proposed research)

---

### 7.2 Andrade & Ferroni (2021)

**Andrade, Philippe, and Filippo Ferroni. "Delphic and Odyssean Monetary Policy Shocks: Evidence from the Euro Area." *Journal of Monetary Economics* 117 (2021): 816–832.**

Applies the Campbell et al. (2012) Delphic/Odyssean distinction to ECB data. Identifies two factors from high-frequency euro-area interest rate surprises: a Delphic factor (central bank forecasts of macro conditions) and an Odyssean factor (commitment to deviate from the normal policy rule). Shows that these two types of shocks have opposite effects on financial conditions and macroeconomic expectations even when they move the yield curve in the same direction — a hawkish surprise that is Delphic (bad news about the economy) tightens financial conditions, while an Odyssean hawkish surprise (commitment to raise rates despite weak outlook) loosens them. Key paper for understanding ECB forward guidance and for identification in a multi-country setting.

- **Identification strategy:** High-frequency factor analysis (intraday changes in OIS rates around ECB press releases and conferences)
- **Key data source:** Euro-area OIS rates; ECB Governing Council announcement windows
- **Main result:** Delphic and Odyssean shocks have opposite effects; ignoring this distinction confounds estimates of forward guidance effectiveness
- **Proximity score:** 4 (methodological and empirical reference for ECB/cross-country analysis)

---

## Category 8: Campbell, Fisher, Justiniano & Melosi (2017)

**Campbell, Jeffrey R., Jonas D.M. Fisher, Alejandro Justiniano, and Leonardo Melosi. "Forward Guidance and Macroeconomic Outcomes since the Financial Crisis." *NBER Macroeconomics Annual* 31 (2017): 283–357.**

Revisits the effectiveness of Federal Reserve forward guidance during 2009–2015 using both event studies and DSGE model analysis. Finds that the FOMC's "extended period" and calendar-date guidance had limited macroeconomic impact when interpreted as Delphic (i.e., merely forecasting continued weakness), but the introduction of the Evans Rule (threshold-based guidance) had larger Odyssean effects. Provides the most detailed retrospective on the Fed's post-crisis forward guidance experience and distinguishes between types of path communication by their effect on the reaction function.

- **Identification strategy:** DSGE model estimation + event study
- **Key data source:** FOMC statements, Blue Chip forecasts, financial market data
- **Main result:** Calendar-date guidance was interpreted as primarily Delphic; threshold-based guidance had larger Odyssean effects; overall forward guidance had modest but positive macroeconomic impact
- **Proximity score:** 5 (direct analysis of rate-path communication episodes; key empirical reference)

---

## Category 9: Emerging Issues — ECB Failure, Inflation Surge, and Reform Proposals

### 9.1 Bernanke (2024) — Bank of England Review

**Bernanke, Ben S. "Forecasting for Monetary Policy Making and Communication at the Bank of England." Bank of England Review, 2024.**

In this landmark external review, Bernanke (the creator of the Fed's dot plot in 2012) recommends that the Bank of England replace its fan-chart forecasts with scenario-based communication, explicitly comparing this favorably to the dot plot. Documents the limitations of both approaches — the dot plot creates false precision about the future rate path, while fan charts obscure the uncertainty around the central forecast. Proposes that publishing alternative scenarios alongside a baseline provides better communication of the central bank's reaction function than either approach. This is the key recent policy document in the reform debate.

- **Identification strategy:** Policy review; comparative institutional analysis
- **Key data source:** Bank of England forecasting records; cross-country comparison
- **Main result:** Scenario-based communication is preferable to point forecasts or dot plots for conveying the conditional nature of policy guidance
- **Proximity score:** 4 (current policy frontier; directly relevant to the "what should replace the dot plot?" question)

---

### 9.2 ECB Forward Guidance Failure (2022) — ECB Occasional Paper No. 290

**ECB. "Rate Forward Guidance in an Environment of Large Central Bank Balance Sheets." ECB Occasional Paper No. 290, 2022.**

Institutional assessment of ECB rate forward guidance following the inflation surge of 2022, in which the ECB was constrained by its "sequencing" guidance (committing to complete asset purchases before raising rates) during a period when rapid rate increases were warranted. Documents that at least two ECB Governing Council members believed the sequencing commitment delayed rate increases by at least one month in 2022. This is the clearest recent case study of forward guidance generating costly over-commitment — the key empirical argument against publishing rate paths.

- **Identification strategy:** Institutional narrative analysis; comparison of projected and realized policy paths
- **Key data source:** ECB Governing Council decisions; OIS curves; ECB inflation projections
- **Main result:** Rate forward guidance created costly over-commitment in 2022; sequencing guidance constrained the ECB during an inflation surge
- **Proximity score:** 4 (key recent case study of the costs of rate-path publication)

### 9.3 Sutherland (2023)

**Sutherland, Matthew. "Forward Guidance and Expectation Formation: A Narrative Approach." *Journal of Applied Econometrics* 38, no. 7 (2023): 1079–1098.**

Constructs a narrative dataset of central bank forward guidance episodes and their attributes for a panel of central banks including the Fed, ECB, Riksbank, Norges Bank, and RBNZ. Finds that forecasters revise interest rate forecasts in the intended direction by an average of 5 basis points following forward guidance changes — a small effect relative to the magnitude of guidance changes, suggesting incomplete expectations formation. Tests for channels: the small average effect is consistent with noise, inattention, or credibility constraints. Provides the most recent cross-country evidence on forward guidance transmission to professional forecasters.

- **Identification strategy:** Narrative identification of forward guidance changes; panel regression with survey forecast revisions
- **Key data source:** BIS forward guidance dataset; Consensus Economics survey forecasts
- **Main result:** Forward guidance moves professional forecasts by only 5 bps on average; incomplete expectations formation is empirically important
- **Proximity score:** 4 (recent cross-country evidence; directly relevant to credibility question)

## Category 10: Methods Papers

### 10.1 Miranda-Agrippino & Ricco (2021)

**Miranda-Agrippino, Silvia, and Giovanni Ricco. "The Transmission of Monetary Policy Shocks." *American Economic Review: Insights* 3, no. 3 (2021): 387–403.**

Documents that standard high-frequency monetary policy surprises are contaminated by the Fed's Greenbook internal forecasts — markets partially anticipate the Fed's private information about the economy. Proposes a "purified" series of monetary policy surprises that controls for this Greenbook information and shows that the resulting estimates of monetary non-neutrality are substantially larger and more stable. Directly relevant to any empirical study of forward guidance shocks: the Greenbook contamination problem applies especially to forward guidance, since the Fed's forward path may reflect its private macro outlook.

- **Identification strategy:** IV with Greenbook-orthogonalized monetary policy surprises
- **Key data source:** Greenbook forecasts (Fed internal), federal funds futures, Blue Chip forecasts
- **Main result:** Greenbook-purified surprises show stronger and more robust monetary non-neutrality; price and activity puzzles disappear
- **Proximity score:** 3 (essential methods reference for any empirical study on this topic)
