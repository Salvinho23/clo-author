# Positioning Recommendation: Forward Guidance and the Policy Rate Path

**Date:** 2026-03-13
**Agent:** librarian

---

## Suggested Contribution Statement

A paper on this topic should position itself at the intersection of two underexplored areas: (1) the *marginal* value of quantitative (numerical) rate-path publication over qualitative forward guidance, and (2) the *asymmetric costs* of over-commitment when the published path proves wrong. The existing literature establishes that forward guidance matters but largely treats the form of guidance — verbal vs. numerical — as secondary. This paper should make the *form* of communication the central object of analysis.

---

## Paper Idea 1: The Marginal Value of Publishing Numbers

**Question:** Does publishing a numerical interest rate path move markets and professional forecasters more than verbal forward guidance of equivalent strength?

**Approach:** Cross-country panel event study exploiting the RBNZ's practice of publishing a rate path only at some meetings (quarterly Monetary Policy Statements) but not others (OCR reviews). The RBNZ design creates a near-random assignment of quantitative vs. qualitative guidance conditional on information content. Apply the Bauer-Swanson (2023) endogeneity correction and Jarociński-Karadi (2020) decomposition to separate Delphic from Odyssean effects.

**Contribution:** First clean identification of the marginal effect of quantitative path publication over verbal guidance, using a within-bank design that holds monetary policy stance approximately constant. This answers the most direct policy question and has immediate relevance to the Fed's debate about the dot plot.

**Data needed:** RBNZ meeting dates, OIS curves (New Zealand), Consensus Economics forecasts for New Zealand, categorization of RBNZ meetings by whether a path was published.

**Target journals:** AEJ:Macro, JME, JEDC. Potentially AER or QJE with a sufficiently large estimated effect and clean identification.

**Scooping risk:** LOW — no paper has done this specific within-RBNZ comparison with modern HFI methods.

## Paper Idea 2: The Cost of Over-Commitment — Evidence from the 2021–2023 Inflation Surge

**Question:** Did central banks that had published explicit rate path commitments (or made equivalent verbal commitments to near-zero rates) respond to the 2021–2023 inflation surge with a statistically significant lag relative to comparable central banks without such commitments?

**Approach:** Cross-country panel regression (quarterly, 2020–2024) of the timing and size of policy rate increases on: (1) a dummy for pre-existing quantitative rate-path commitment, (2) the deviation of realized inflation from the path-implied inflation trajectory, and (3) controls for inflation gap, output gap, and central bank independence. Supplement with event studies around the first rate increase to test whether path-publishing central banks received larger negative market surprises (consistent with delayed response).

**Contribution:** First systematic empirical estimate of the over-commitment cost using the 2022 "test" as a natural quasi-experiment. The ECB sequencing guidance, the Riksbank's 2021 commitment to hold rates near zero, and the Fed's "transitory" guidance all provide variation in the *ex ante* degree of commitment.

**Data needed:** Policy rate data (BIS, central bank websites), CPI and output data (IMF, OECD), forward guidance commitment indicators (narrative coding from Sutherland 2023 or original coding), OIS curves for market-implied expectations.

**Target journals:** AEJ:Macro, JME, AER (if the welfare cost is quantifiable). Also relevant for AEJ:Policy given the direct policy implications.

**Scooping risk:** MEDIUM — several ECB occasional papers and BIS working papers have discussed the ECB's guidance failure qualitatively, but no systematic cross-country regression exists.

## Paper Idea 3: Scenario-Based Communication as a Reform

**Question:** Does scenario-based central bank communication (conditioning on alternative macroeconomic paths rather than publishing a single rate path) convey more information to financial markets while reducing over-commitment costs?

**Approach:** Take advantage of the Norges Bank's practice of publishing fan charts and alternative scenarios alongside its rate path, which creates natural variation in the uncertainty communicated. Also leverage the Bank of England's implementation of Bernanke's (2024) recommendation to publish alternative scenarios. Design a text analysis of central bank communications to measure the "commitment content" of different communication formats and relate this to the information content (yield curve movements) and credibility cost (revision frequency) of the guidance.

**Contribution:** The first empirical evaluation of scenario-based vs. point-estimate rate guidance. Directly relevant to the ongoing post-Bernanke debate about reforming the Bank of England's, and potentially the Fed's, communication framework.

**Data needed:** Norges Bank MPR fan chart data; Bank of England post-reform communications (2025–); OIS curves; text data from MPR releases.

**Target journals:** JME, AEJ:Macro, IJCB. Policy relevance is high; may also be publishable in economic policy venues (e.g., Economic Policy, Oxford Economic Papers).

**Scooping risk:** LOW — the Bank of England reform is too recent (announced 2024, implementing 2025) for any paper to have been written. This is an open window.

## Recommended Starting Point

**Paper Idea 1** (marginal value of quantitative guidance) is the most tractable because: (1) it has a clean within-country identification design (RBNZ); (2) it requires only publicly available data; (3) it is methodologically grounded in established HFI methods; and (4) it directly answers the most policy-relevant question — whether the Fed's dot plot provides incremental value over its existing verbal communication.

The 2022–2023 inflation episode provides compelling motivation: if quantitative path publication provides little *upside* (marginal value is small) while creating substantial *downside* (over-commitment costs during the inflation surge), the case for the dot plot becomes very weak. This framing matches the current policy debate and would be of immediate interest to any AEJ:Macro or JME referee.
