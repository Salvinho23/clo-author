## 1. Where the Theoretical Debate Stands

### The Foundational Case for Publication (1990s–2000s)

The theoretical case for publishing the rate path rests on three pillars. First, from Kydland-Prescott and Barro-Gordon: commitment devices improve on discretion by eliminating the inflation bias. Second, from Woodford (2003): monetary policy works primarily through expectations about the *future* rate path, so communicating that path should improve transmission. Third, from Eggertsson and Woodford (2003): at the zero lower bound, committing to a future rate path is the *only* effective tool, making path publication both necessary and sufficient for additional stimulus.

These three pillars generated a strong theoretical consensus in the 2000s in favor of forward guidance, and specifically in favor of quantitative (numerical) rate-path publication as the most precise form of commitment.

### The Forward Guidance Puzzle (2012–2018): Questioning How Powerful the Tool Is

Del Negro, Giannoni and Patterson (2023, circulated 2012) delivered a shock to the consensus: standard DSGE models wildly overpredict the power of forward guidance. If the models are right, publishing a path promising low rates for two years should cause a near-boom; empirically, the effects are an order of magnitude smaller. This opened a literature on resolving the puzzle:

- **Incomplete markets (McKay-Nakamura-Steinsson 2016):** Precautionary savings dampen the response to future rate promises; the puzzle is attenuated in HANK models.
- **Lack of common knowledge (Angeletos-Lian 2018):** Higher-order uncertainty erodes the general equilibrium amplification; the puzzle is attenuated and grows with the horizon of the guidance.
- **Durable goods (McKay-Wieland 2022):** Durable spending responds to the current user cost (real rate), not the cumulative future rate path; forward guidance at long horizons has minimal durable goods effects.
- **Bounded rationality/adaptive learning (Angeletos-Sastry 2021, Eusepi et al. 2022):** Agents who do not perfectly solve intertemporal problems respond weakly to distant promises.

The current theoretical consensus is that forward guidance is *less* powerful than the benchmark DSGE model implies, with the magnitude of attenuation depending on which friction is present. This weakens the *quantitative* case for rate-path publication without eliminating the *qualitative* case.

### The Neo-Fisherian Challenge (2015–present)

Cochrane (2018) and associated work pose a deeper challenge: at the zero lower bound, the standard NK model requires off-equilibrium threats to select the stable equilibrium — threats that no central bank has ever actually carried out. Under the fiscal theory of the price level, extended low rates will eventually *lower* inflation, directly contradicting the "lower for longer" logic. The neo-Fisherian critique has not displaced the standard view in policy circles, but it has reinforced skepticism about the theoretical foundations of long-horizon commitment.

### The Information Effects Complication (2018–present)

Nakamura and Steinsson (2018) demonstrated that FOMC announcements release two types of information simultaneously: pure policy signals and the Fed's private information about the state of the economy. A hawkish forward guidance announcement may raise rates *and* raise stock prices because markets interpret it as good economic news (the "Delphic" channel). Jarociński and Karadi (2020) developed a structural decomposition to separate these two components. This complication is especially acute for rate-path publication: a published rate path may be read as either a commitment (Odyssean) or a forecast (Delphic), and the two interpretations have opposite implications for financial conditions.

## 2. Where the Empirical Debate Stands

### Early-Adopter Evidence (2009–2020)

The three original rate-path publishers — RBNZ (1997), Norges Bank (2005), and Riksbank (2007) — provide the primary empirical laboratory. The evidence is broadly consistent:

- Published paths are moderately predictable (40–75% of variance explained by market information the day before).
- Market rates adjust significantly to path surprises but far less than one-for-one; credibility falls with horizon.
- For the RBNZ, meetings with a published path generate very similar market reactions to meetings with only verbal guidance — the *marginal* contribution of quantitative path information is small.
- The Riksbank provides a cautionary tale: during 2010–2014, the Riksbank maintained a high published path to signal its "leaning against the wind" policy while the market expected (correctly) that rates would be cut — the published path was systematically wrong for strategic reasons, damaging its credibility.

### The Fed Dot Plot (2012–present)

The Fed's dot plot provides the most studied recent case of rate-path publication. Key empirical findings:

- The dot plot moves markets: Swanson (2021) demonstrates that forward guidance shocks have effects comparable in magnitude to conventional rate changes.
- The dot plot is subject to large forecast errors: during the 2021–2023 inflation surge, the dot plot failed dramatically, with the median 2023 year-end rate prediction off by hundreds of basis points.
- Internal FOMC disagreement (measured by the dot plot's variance) has risen since 2022, reducing the signal value of the median dot.
- Post-Bernanke review (2024), there is active policy debate about replacing the dot plot with scenario-based communication.

### High-Frequency Identification Progress (2016–2023)

The methodology for identifying forward guidance shocks has advanced substantially. The Bauer-Swanson (2023) correction for the "Fed response to news" channel shows that naive HFI estimates are biased downward by 2–4x. The Miranda-Agrippino and Ricco (2021) Greenbook-purification yields similar conclusions. These methodological advances imply that forward guidance effects are substantially larger than earlier studies found — a point in favor of the policy relevance of rate-path publication.

### Cross-Country Evidence (2015–2024)

Cross-country evidence on guidance effectiveness remains limited. Sutherland's (2023) narrative dataset shows that professional forecasters revise rate expectations by only 5 basis points on average in response to guidance changes — a disappointingly small effect given the scale of guidance episodes. The ECB's 2022 sequencing guidance failure provides the most striking recent example of over-commitment costs.

## 3. What Empirical Gaps Remain

### Gap 1: Isolating the Marginal Effect of the Published Number

No paper cleanly identifies the *marginal* effect of publishing a numerical rate path *conditional on all other communication*. The closest is Andersson-Hofmann's RBNZ comparison of meetings with and without path publication, but this study predates recent methodological advances (Bauer-Swanson correction, Jarociński-Karadi decomposition) and covers only one central bank with relatively coarse event design.

### Gap 2: Estimating the Costs of Over-Commitment

The theoretical costs of over-commitment are well-articulated (Mishkin, Feroli et al.) but empirically quantified only through case studies (Riksbank 2010–2014, ECB 2022). No paper has estimated the welfare cost of over-commitment in a systematic, cross-country regression framework.

### Gap 3: Credibility Heterogeneity Across Central Banks

Sutherland (2023) establishes that forward guidance effects on forecaster expectations are small on average, but does not exploit heterogeneity in institutional credibility. High-credibility central banks (e.g., Riksbank before 2010, Norges Bank) should show larger alignment between published paths and market expectations than low-credibility banks.

### Gap 4: The Reaction Function Communication Problem

Feroli et al. (2017) argue that publishing a rate *level* path is less effective than publishing the *reaction function*. No systematic cross-country test of this hypothesis exists. The Bernanke (2024) recommendation for scenario-based communication is a related proposal that has not yet been evaluated empirically.

### Gap 5: Survey-Based Evidence on Market Interpretation

Almost no work uses survey data (e.g., Consensus Economics, Reuters polls) to test whether markets interpret published rate paths as Odyssean (commitment) or Delphic (forecast), or how this interpretation shifts with communication framing.

### Gap 6: Post-2020 High-Inflation Episode

The 2021–2023 inflation surge provides a natural experiment: central banks that had published rate paths (and committed to near-zero rates "for an extended period") were arguably constrained in their ability to respond. No systematic empirical evaluation of this claim exists using the modern HFI toolkit.

## 4. Where a New Paper Fits

The most productive entry point for a new paper is **Gap 1** combined with **Gap 6**: using the 2021–2023 episode as an exogenous variation in the *salience* and *bindingness* of published rate paths, with high-frequency identification to separate the signaling effects (beneficial) from the commitment costs (harmful).

Alternatively, a **cross-country panel event study** exploiting variation in whether a central bank publishes a numerical path (vs. verbal guidance only) — using the Bauer-Swanson corrected HFI methodology — would provide the first clean estimate of the marginal value of quantitative over qualitative guidance.

The Bernanke (2024) review also opens a new angle: **scenario-based communication as an alternative to the dot plot**, which could be evaluated using the Bank of England's recent reform as a natural experiment.
